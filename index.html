About

I grew up between more than 15 countries, absorbing mathematics the way other kids absorb football. My father is a mathematician. Abstraction was never scary — it was just dinner conversation.

Before finishing high school, I was already a research collaborator at the Statistics and Data Science Lab (LED–UFAL), auditing undergraduate math courses, and competing in national olympiads. Not because I had to — because I found it genuinely enjoyable

Today I study Applied Mathematics at FGV/EMAp (full scholarship), where the curriculum forces you to actually understand things: not just implement gradient descent, but derive it. I implement algorithms from scratch in C and C++. I know what a B-tree is and I've benchmarked one.

I want to work with teams that care about correctness, performance, and craft. I'm available for backend development, data engineering, technical tutoring, or anything at the intersection of math and software.

Inverted Index

Full implementation of an Inverted Index in C++ — the data structure behind every search engine. The interesting part: a comparative benchmark of three different tree structures (BST, AVL, B-tree) measuring build time, query latency, and memory footprint across corpuses of increasing size. Not just "it works" — but why one is faster than the other under specific conditions.

Data Structures BST AVL Tree B-Tree Benchmarking

K-Nearest Neighbors — from scratch

KNN implemented in plain C with no ML libraries. The focus was on performance and memory efficiency: manual memory management, cache-aware data layout, and profiling to understand where cycles actually go. A useful exercise in understanding what the abstractions of numpy or sklearn are actually doing underneath.

Implementation of Hessenberg reduction routines for orthogonal matrices and spectral analysis of the resulting forms — part of a Numerical Linear Algebra course at FGV/EMAp. Applied to understand eigenvalue stability and the numerical behaviour of orthogonal transformations.

Exploration of how standard matrix metrics — condition number, spectral radius, singular value distribution — behave as Gaussian random matrices grow in dimension. Reveals the counterintuitive geometry of high-dimensional probability, including concentration of measure phenomena.
